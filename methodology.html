How FormFactor works

The research behind FormFactor

Keep a neutral spine under load

Stuart McGill's spine research strongly advises against rounding the lower back (spinal flexion) under load. Rounding, especially at the L4-L5 and L5-S1 joints, puts large shear forces and stress on the lumbar discs. Lifters should keep their natural lower-back curve through the whole lift, and the squat should be hip-dominant: push the hips back and lean the chest forward just enough to use the glutes and hamstrings, without leaning too far. [1, 2, 7, 8]

What spinal shear force is

Shear force happens when a force pushes along the spine, parallel to the discs, so neighboring vertebrae try to slide past each other. It is mainly caused by leaning forward under load, rounding the lower back, and twisting under load. Rounding under heavy load presses unevenly on the front of the discs, while arching too far (hyperextension) jams the facet joints together. [7, 9]

Some spine movement is normal

Aasa and colleagues measured the lumbar spine during squats and deadlifts. Even when lifters tried to keep a neutral spine, the spine still moved: about 9–12° in the upper lumbar spine and 18–22° in the lower lumbar spine. That is far from full flexion (around 60–65°), so it is better to think of a safe "neutral zone" than a spine that never moves. [3]

Trunk lean depends on your body

A healthy squat leans the torso forward. How much depends on leg proportions: lifters with longer thigh bones need more forward lean to keep the bar over mid-foot, while shorter thigh bones allow a more upright torso. A typical range is about 15°–45° of trunk lean from vertical. [5, 6, 11]

The real hazard: the back angle changing mid-lift

The danger comes when the back angle changes during the lift: the chest collapses forward, or the hips shoot up out of the bottom of the squat. Then the load moves from the legs to the spine. [6, 10] This is why FormFactor measures angular velocity (how fast an angle changes, Δθ / Δt) and looks for two concerning patterns: sudden spikes, and big differences between upper-body and lower-body movement. Warning levels are set by comparing good lifts with poor-form lifts, with research as support.

Trunk vs. shin

Straub and Powers (2024) reviewed squat biomechanics and showed that comparing trunk lean to shin lean tells you whether a squat works the hips or the knees more (see section 3 below). [4]

1. Finding the body

FormFactor uses Google's MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (the "full" model) to find 33 body points in every camera frame. All of this runs inside your browser. The camera video is never uploaded or saved. (The browser downloads the model file from Google once.)

From the side, one side of the body is closer to the camera. FormFactor uses whichever side MediaPipe can see more clearly, and tracks four joints on it: shoulder, hip, knee, and ankle.

2. Deciding safe, warning, or danger

Research on spine loading (Stuart McGill's work) points to rounding or sudden changes in back angle under load as the main hazard: when the chest collapses forward or the hips shoot up out of the bottom of a squat, load moves from the legs to the lower back. So FormFactor's rules are:

  • Danger: the trunk tips forward very fast.
  • Shear force warning: the knees straighten while the chest is still dropping forward (the hips rise first, a "good morning" squat pattern).
  • Warning (squats only): trunk leans more than 45° forward. Research gives a typical safe range of about 15°–45° off vertical. Because the right amount of lean depends on leg length, this is only a warning.
  • Rep-to-rep changes (squats only): the right squat depth depends on the person, so FormFactor doesn't judge depth against a fixed number. Instead it measures each rep's depth (the thigh angle at the bottom: 0° = thigh level, positive = hip below knee) and its trunk − shin difference, and compares every rep to your first rep. A rep that is more than 10° less deep, or whose trunk − shin shifts by more than 10°, gets a warning, since changes during a set can be a sign of fatigue.

The speed limits are starting estimates that I am tuning by recording good and poor lifts and comparing them.

3. Trunk vs. shin (muscle emphasis, squats)

FormFactor measures trunk inclination (shoulder-to-hip line) and shin inclination (ankle-to-knee line), both as degrees forward from vertical, using the same convention for every frame. Each angle is smoothed over about 0.2 seconds so one inaccurate dot can't trigger feedback. It then compares them: trunk − shin.

Using the categories summarized by Straub and Powers (2024):

  • Hip-biased: trunk angle exceeds shin angle by more than about 10°. Associated with relatively greater hip-extensor demand.
  • Approximately balanced: the angles differ by about 10° or less. Hip and knee extensor demands are more similar.
  • Knee-biased: shin angle exceeds trunk angle by more than about 10°. Associated with relatively greater knee-extensor demand.

FormFactor reports the category at the bottom of each rep and charts the difference through the whole set (descent, bottom, and back up). This explains how the work is shared between hips and knees. It is not an injury risk or a form failure. These categories come from squat research, so they are shown for squats only.

Honest limitations

  • One 2D camera can't see the lower back's curve. FormFactor sees a straight line from shoulder to hip. Someone can lean forward with a flat back, or round their back without that line moving much. Aasa et al. measured 9–22° of lumbar motion in squats and deadlifts [3]; FormFactor cannot measure this.
  • No forces. FormFactor does not know the weight on the bar, so it cannot measure force in Newtons. Results are angle-based estimates only.
  • Camera setup matters. Best results need a true side view, the whole body in frame, and good lighting. If the body is hard to see, FormFactor says so.
  • Checked against published photos. On the three example photos in Straub & Powers (2024), Figure 5, FormFactor put all three in the same category as the paper, with angles within 1°–4°. See VALIDATION.md.
  • Not validated clinically. FormFactor has not been tested against lab motion-capture equipment.
